Understanding Confidence Intervals

2.1 The big idea

2.1.1 Confidence interval

In short. A confidence interval is a range of values that comes from a sample. It is used to estimate an unknown value in the population, like the population mean. the confidence interval is written with a minimum and then a maximum, such as “11.0 to 13.0”. It always comes with a confidence level, such as 95%.

What “confident” really means The confidence is about the method used to build the interval, not about any one interval. Here is what it really means:

  1. There is one true value. Somewhere in the population there is a real value we want to know, such as the average hours of sleep for all students at a university. It is fixed, but we cannot see it, because we cannot measure everyone.
  2. Take one sample and build an interval. We collect a random sample, calculate the sample mean, then add and subtract the margin of error that gives us just one interval.
  3. Imagine repeating those steps. Imagine we take a new sample, and repeat the exact same steps. The people would be different in a new sample, so the sample mean should be a little different, same goes for the newly calculated interval.
  4. Repeat the steps. Actually repeat the steps hundreds of times, leaving you with hundreds of intervals. Most of the intervals will contain the true value, only a few would miss it and that can be chalked up to bad luck in who happened to be sampled.
  5. The confidence level is the percentage of correct hits from that pile of intervals. A 95% confidence level means the method used to build the interval was built so that around 95 intervals out of 100 intervals completed contain the true value. This means about 5 would miss. (The reason this complicated pattern is predictable is the sampling distribution.)
  6. We only get one interval in real life scenarios. In day to day statistics we will not have done all 100 sample draws, which means we are not sure if our interval is one of the 95 hits or one of the 5 misses. The 95% represents how often the method works, to put it another way before any data collection is done our CI will have a 95% chance of containing the true vale. After collection it becomes a 0% or 100% chance that it captured the true value, we don’t know for certain if it is or isn’t.

2.1.4 Population

The complete set of people, events or items that a study is about. We can typically never draw conclusions about a population, which is why we take samples. This limitation is the main reason our statistics are inferential

2.1.5 Sample

Samples are a small subset of a larger set of data, that larger set being the population. The sample results allow us to draw inferences about the population. Confidence intervals assume the sample was chosen at random (called probability sampling), so that every member of the population had a fair chance of being picked. Without random sampling the results would not be generalizable to the population, and therefore not usable in inferential statistics.

###Parameter {#parameter}

A parameter is a number that describes a whole population, such as the population mean (written μ). It is fixed but usually unknown.

###Statistic {#statistic}

A statistic is a number calculated from a sample, such as the sample mean (written x̄). We can see it, but it changes from sample to sample.

A confidence interval uses a statistic to estimate a parameter.

2.1.6 Sample mean

The “average” of the values in one sample. A mean is calculated by summing the scores together divided by the total number of scores. It is a single number, and it is our best guess at what the population mean looks like. Another sample would give a different sample mean. See also sampling distribution vs. sample mean.

2.1.7 Standard deviation

A measure of how spread out the individual values are around the mean. A standard deviation that is close to zero means the data points sit close to the mean. A larger SD means they are more varied and spread out.
